Abstract
Thermally modified wood is primarily used in exterior applications due to its enhanced
resistance to biotic degradation. However, reduced mechanical performance limits its
structural use. This study investigates the structural potential of high-temperature-treated
European beech timber (Fagus sylvatica, L.) and evaluates its mechanical properties and
grading models for structural design. Timber from 32 beech logs was air-dried and divided
into untreated (NoTMW) and thermally modified (TMW) groups. Thermal modification
was carried out commercially in an oxidizing atmosphere at 190 ◦C. All specimens were
visually graded according to DIN 4074-5 and assessed using acoustic non-destructive
methods before testing in four-point bending following EN 408. Modulus of elasticity
(MOE), modulus of rupture (MOR), and density were determined, and characteristic values
were calculated according to EN 384. On average, TMW exhibited a 17% reduction in
bending strength compared to untreated wood, while both static and dynamic MOE were
not significantly affected. The multiple regression model only slightly improved bending
strength prediction compared with single linear regression based on global modulus, as
the R2-value increased from 17% to 19%. The prediction of stiffness of thermally treated
beech timber was greatly improved by combining local and acoustic moduli, explaining
76% of the total variation.
Keywords: thermally modified wood; beech; structural timber; bending strength;
non-destructive testing; strength grading
1. Introduction
In Europe, interest in hardwood construction materials is increasing due to limited
softwood availability, substantial hardwood resources, and policies promoting the refor-
estation of mixed forests to improve resilience to changing soil and climatic conditions [1].
In addition, projections suggest that increasing pressure from biotic and abiotic stressors on
softwoods may further raise the share of beech in European forests. These trends provide a
strong rationale for exploring the use of beech wood in structural applications.
Thermally modified wood (TMW) is produced by heating wood at 160–230 ◦C for
several hours in a controlled atmosphere, which degrades hemicelluloses, reduces free –OH
groups and induces lignin cross-linking, thereby lowering equilibrium moisture content,
improving dimensional stability, and increasing resistance to fungal decay while imparting
a darker color [2].

2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Material Selection and Preparation
Stems from 16 beech trees were harvested in the Zvolen district, Slovakia. Mid-length
diameter ranged from 430 mm to 600 mm and length from 6.2 m to 7.2 m. From each stem,
two logs were obtained by cross-cutting. Boards with dimensions of 60 × 160 × 3200 mm
were sawn from the logs using a segmental sawing pattern. The dimensions were based on
the final reference height of 150 mm. To enable comparison between treated and untreated
boards, special board selection was carried out; two neighboring boards were taken for
paired comparison (Figure 1). Untreated timber specimens were described by [19]. The
boards were stacked in cages and air-dried for 1 year. Subsequently, visual strength grading
of the timber was carried out according to DIN 4074-5, and only boards meeting at least
strength grade LS7 were included in the test set [20].
Figure 1. Segmental sawing pattern and adjacent-board pairing for thermally modified wood (TMW)
and untreated wood (NoTMW).
The graded timber was thermally modified. The boards were industrially treated by
the company Designwood at 190 ◦C for 3.5 h in an oxidative air atmosphere. The selected
temperature lies in the high end of the temperature range where the apparent strength
of beech treated wood remained unchanged [5,21]. The thermal modification process
included drying to a moisture content (MC) of approximately 4%, the thermal treatment
itself, and cooling combined with conditioning. The test specimens were then stored in
a roofed warehouse, where they reached equilibrium moisture content, and they were
visually regraded again according to DIN 4074-5 [20].
For testing, the boards were re-sized and squared to the final dimensions of
50 × 150 × 3100 mm (b × h × l). The number of tested thermally modified and ref-
erence boards was 53 and 53, respectively, resulting in 34 paired samples. Due to erroneous
readings, five ultrasound speed measurements were excluded, resulting in three missing
paired observations. In addition, two natural-frequency measurements were excluded,

As confirmed by paired tests, all types of measured moduli were equal or slightly
higher in treated wood but nonsignificant when compared to untreated timber. This
suggests that the NDT parameters did not affect the treatment. Despite the clear difference
in strength, thermally untreated structural timbers are almost as stiff as treated ones.
Density is about 5% lower in the TMW group, with the same value of the coefficient of
variation in both groups. Assuming a similar swelling coefficient [27], the oven-dry density
of treated beech wood changed by about 2.4%, which is in a reasonable range for the given
treatment temperature and time of treatment (190 ◦C, 3.5 h) [21,28]. A modest but consistent
reduction in material density is associated with treatment intensity.
Interestingly, the bending strength distribution of TMW boards showed two peaks
compared to NoTMW strength (Figure 3a). The results of cumulative distribution suggest that
thermal treatment reduced the strength of lower-quality wood, while it may maintain—or,
in some cases, slightly improve—the strength of higher-quality wood. This contributed to
the increased scatter in strength values compared to the untreated cumulative distribution
(Figure 3b).
